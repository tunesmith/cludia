# The Ninth Room

## How to present the mystery

This is a six-part fair-play mystery intended for incremental analysis. Present one numbered part at a time. After each part, let the solver record clues, construct hypotheses, and ask clarification questions before continuing.

Do not provide the separate epilogue until the solver has read Part VI and either made an accusation or explicitly requested the solution.

Evidence follows these rules:

- Neutral narration of observable conditions is reliable.
- Anything attributed to a character remains testimony unless independently corroborated.
- Documents reliably contain the words described, but their authors and contents may be deceptive.
- No secret passage, disguised body, supernatural event, or unintroduced culprit is involved.
- After Part VI, every fact needed for the intended solution has appeared.

## Part I: The Locked Study

Vesper House stood on a low tidal island off the village of Fallowmere. A stone causeway connected it to the mainland whenever the tide permitted. Fallowmere had a railway station with passenger platforms, a parcels office, a telegraph counter, and a freight yard. Greyhaven, one stop inland, was the nearest town.

On October 18, 1924, six guests crossed the causeway to dine with Aldous Bell, a collector of rare books:

- **Miriam Bell**, Aldous's niece and a pharmaceutical chemist.
- **Felix Harrow**, his private secretary.
- **Dr. Lenora Pike**, his physician and oldest friend.
- **Gideon Rusk**, solicitor to the Bell estate.
- **Cassian Vale**, an illusionist whose apparatus Aldous collected.
- **Beatrice Orr**, curator of the Vesper collection.

Constable Merritt was also at the house. He had arrived before dinner to return the effects of Ruth Dallow, Beatrice's assistant cataloguer, and to ask Aldous some final questions about her death. Merritt was not one of the six guests. The tide covered the causeway before he could leave.

Ruth had died three nights earlier beneath a freight wagon at Fallowmere Station. The preliminary finding was accidental death.

At dinner, Aldous placed an unopened parcel beside his chair and addressed the table:

> "One object in my collection is false. One account in my ledger is false. And someone at this table made a corpse of Ruth Dallow. At half past ten, I shall name the object, the account, and the killer."

At 9:05, Aldous carried the parcel upstairs to his study, known as the Ninth Room because its door bore a brass numeral IX. He told the housekeeper, Mrs. Wren:

> "No one comes in until I ring twice."

Mrs. Wren watched him lock the door from inside.

At 9:18, the study's handbell rang once. Mrs. Wren carried up the hot water Aldous normally requested in the evening. Before she knocked, his voice came through the door:

> "Leave it, Wren. I shall ring twice when I am ready."

She left the tray on a corridor table. It remained there untouched.

At 10:30, Aldous had not appeared. The door was still locked, and its only key could be seen in the inside of the keyhole. The windows were latched. Iron bars crossed the chimney. Felix and Cassian broke down the door.

Aldous was dead at his desk.

There was no obvious weapon. His right hand pressed against his chest. At the fleshy base of his left thumb was a tiny puncture surrounded by a faint blue-black stain.

The parcel lay open. It had contained a gray vellum book entitled *A Dissertation Concerning the Tides*, supposedly printed in 1681. Its brass clasp stood open. Only the first sixteen leaves had been separated along their folded edges; the remaining leaves were still uncut.

Aldous's spectacles were folded inside their case. His ivory paper knife remained sheathed on the opposite side of the room.

His desk clock had stopped at 9:27. Its glass was intact, but the winding key would not turn.

A fresh scrape ran from the chair's ordinary position toward the desk. The chair's back rail now rested against the desk directly beneath the clock.

On the cold hearth lay a burned fragment of paper bearing the surviving text:

> **ROW—17**

Beneath Aldous's right hand was an unfinished sentence:

> "The mistake was assuming that Ruth had discovered—"

No one admitted sending the parcel.

## Part II: The Sixteenth Page

Dr. Pike examined Aldous while the others remained downstairs.

The puncture on his thumb was recent. The surrounding blue-black color was not bruising but a foreign dye. His posture and the condition of his pupils were consistent with aconitine poisoning, although Dr. Pike could not prove that diagnosis without chemical analysis.

Introduced through a puncture, aconitine might leave a victim capable of speaking for several minutes. Death could follow ten to thirty minutes later through disruption of the heart.

Dr. Pike also disclosed that Aldous had long suffered from an irregular heartbeat. A severe attack could imitate some signs of poisoning, but it would not explain the puncture or dye.

Mrs. Wren supplied another fact about the clock. At approximately 6:12 that evening, she had noticed that the study clock showed exactly six. Aldous told her not to correct it. If it remained twelve minutes slow, the displayed time of 9:27 represented 9:39.

Beatrice explained the uncut leaves. Printed sheets were traditionally folded into gatherings, and their outer folds sometimes remained joined until a reader opened them with a paper knife. A genuinely uncut seventeenth-century copy would now be valuable precisely because no owner had opened it. Aldous would ordinarily inspect such a book carefully before cutting a single additional leaf.

The book did not behave like an untouched antique. Its first sixteen leaves looked older than the block behind them. Its spine was unusually thick, and the parcel had surprised Aldous by its weight.

On the inside of the brass clasp, Cassian found a narrow groove stained with the same blue-black substance found on Aldous's thumb. The groove was too precisely machined to be seventeenth-century work. Something slender had occupied it, but the groove was empty.

When pressed shut, the clasp resisted briefly and then snapped closed with disproportionate force.

Cassian said:

> "There is a return spring inside it. Something was meant to emerge and then hide itself again."

The parcel had been delivered at 4:00 by a Fallowmere carrier. Its wrapper bore the printed label of **March & Rowe, Antiquarian Booksellers**.

Gideon observed that March & Rowe had ceased trading five years earlier. Beatrice replied that Aldous had purchased a bundle of the firm's old labels when its premises were cleared. They were stored with packing materials in the Bell estate office at Fallowmere. Felix and Gideon both used that office; Beatrice sometimes collected labels there for museum shipments.

Felix had accepted the parcel at Vesper House. There was no invoice, sender's address, or covering letter.

Felix remembered Aldous's reaction when he handed it over. Aldous weighed it in his hands and said:

> "There is something inside this book which the sender does not expect me to read."

The parcel was still unopened when he spoke.

## Part III: The Alibi and the Missing Ampoule

Constable Merritt reconstructed the movements between 9:05 and 10:30.

Gideon and Cassian began playing billiards at approximately 9:10. Felix entered at 9:25 to deliver Gideon a telegram and saw both men.

Merritt then sat in the hall opposite the billiard room from 9:25 until 9:50 while reviewing Ruth's effects. The billiard room had one door, which remained open. Merritt had an uninterrupted view of it; neither Gideon nor Cassian left during that interval. Mrs. Wren saw both men inside shortly before 10:00.

Their presence in the billiard room from 9:25 through 9:50 may therefore be treated as established rather than merely mutually asserted.

Miriam said she had worked alone in her laboratory from shortly after nine until Dr. Pike joined her at approximately 9:40. Dr. Pike confirmed remaining with Miriam until shortly after ten.

Felix said that, apart from delivering the telegram, he had been typing in the library. Beatrice said she had been checking shelf records in the catalogue room. Neither had a continuous witness.

At approximately 9:40, Mrs. Wren passed beneath the Ninth Room and heard a short scraping sound overhead, like a chair driven abruptly across the floor. She heard no cry.

Miriam inspected her locked poison cabinet. One sealed ampoule of aconitine was missing. Her inventory recorded it as present on September 30.

Miriam's current key remained on her watch chain. A duplicate had existed. Aldous borrowed it in June for an inventory of hazardous materials and later told Miriam that it had been lost. The lock showed no damage.

Cassian then described an illusion he had designed years earlier, the **Self-Concealing Thorn**. A spring projected a fine needle and immediately withdrew it into an apparently solid fitting. The only mechanism Cassian personally built had been dismantled after injuring a stagehand.

The plans survived because they had been exhibits in a 1918 patent dispute. Cassian retained his originals. Aldous received a reference copy because he financed the case. Gideon, as solicitor, kept a working copy. Felix had catalogued Aldous's litigation papers during the previous winter and therefore had handled Aldous's copy.

Four people had consequently possessed either the plans or direct access to them: Cassian, Aldous, Gideon, and Felix.

The telegram Felix delivered to Gideon at 9:25 read:

> RETAINED COPY OF DALLOW MESSAGE LOCATED. SIGNATURE TYPED. WILL BRING AFTER TIDE.  
> —FALLOWMERE STATIONMASTER

Gideon said the stationmaster had contacted him because he represented the Bell estate.

## Part IV: Ruth's Last Errand

Ruth did not live at Vesper House. She lodged in Greyhaven and normally travelled one stop by train to Fallowmere before continuing across the causeway to work.

On October 15, she left Greyhaven carrying a return ticket. The outward portion had been used; the portion for travelling home remained in her handbag after her death.

A porter at Fallowmere Station remembered Ruth entering the parcels office at approximately 8:20. She showed him a typed message:

> MISS DALLOW—COME TO THE PARCELS OFFICE AT 8:20. BRING YOUR CATALOGUE NOTES.  
> —A.B.

Aldous denied sending it when Merritt questioned him the following day.

The stationmaster's retained copy matched the porter's recollection. The typed capital **M** sat slightly above the surrounding letters. The typewriter in the Bell estate office at Fallowmere had the same defect. Felix used that machine for routine estate correspondence; Gideon used it for legal and financial business.

The porter heard Ruth arguing with a man in the foggy freight yard shortly before her death. He could not identify the voice. He distinctly remembered Ruth saying:

> "You have mistaken the number. I was examining the row, not the account."

Ruth's body was found beside a freight siding behind the parcels office, not beside a passenger platform. Freight wagons were being moved slowly back and forth there to assemble a train. Ruth was severely short-sighted, yet her spectacles were folded in her handbag.

Among her effects was a catalogue notebook. Its last page had been torn out, but impressions remained on the page beneath:

> ROW 17  
> BLUE FIBRES IN PAPER  
> NOT CONTEMPORARY  
> ASK B.O. BEFORE TELLING A.B.

Row 17 of the Vesper collection contained the **Vesper Psalter**, supposedly produced in the fifteenth century.

Miriam examined an exposed paper edge with a laboratory lens and confirmed that it contained blue fibers of a kind ordinarily associated with modern machine-made paper.

Beatrice objected to opening the Psalter. She claimed that modern repair paper had been introduced during an earlier restoration and that Ruth knew this.

Beatrice had an apparent motive to silence Ruth. Miriam controlled the poison. Cassian had designed the mechanism. Felix had handled the parcel and commonly used the relevant typewriter. Dr. Pike knew both Aldous's heart condition and how aconitine might imitate it. Gideon controlled the estate's legal records.

Nothing yet placed any one of them at the center of all three mysteries.

## Part V: Two Seventeens

Confronted with Ruth's notebook, Beatrice withdrew her restoration story.

Three years earlier, she had badly damaged the original Vesper Psalter during an unauthorized chemical treatment. Terrified of professional disgrace, she constructed a facsimile and substituted it in Row 17. Ruth discovered the modern fibers on October 15 and confronted her. Beatrice begged for one night in which to confess to Aldous herself.

Beatrice could not have met Ruth at Fallowmere that evening. From 7:45 until after 9:00, she dined at Vesper House with Aldous and Dr. Pike. Mrs. Wren served them repeatedly, and the causeway and train timetable made a journey to the 8:20 meeting impossible.

The false object had been identified. Attention shifted to Aldous's private ledger.

It contained an account headed:

> **ESCROW—17: SPECIAL ACQUISITIONS**

Substantial sums had entered the account and then left through supposed payments to March & Rowe. Two payments were dated after the firm ceased trading. No corresponding books or manuscripts appeared in Beatrice's catalogues.

Every payment authorization bore Aldous's signature, but the signatures were perfectly identical, including a small failure of ink in the same place. They had been made with Aldous's rubber signature stamp.

The stamp was stored in the Fallowmere estate office. Felix and Gideon both used it legitimately.

Escrow 17 was not part of the ordinary bookkeeping Felix maintained. The account agreement named only Aldous and Gideon. Bank statements were sent under seal to Gideon's chambers, then forwarded to Aldous for his private ledger. Before Ruth's death, no other guest was shown to have known that the account was numbered seventeen.

Gideon acknowledged administering the account. He said Aldous had ordered him to conceal purchases from rival collectors by using March & Rowe's old name. The money had been withdrawn through bearer drafts, so the bank could not identify the person who ultimately received it.

The most recent Escrow 17 bank statement was missing from Aldous's ledger. A narrow scorched strip remained where the page had been torn from its binding.

Merritt compared that strip with the burned fragment from the Ninth Room. The paper, ruling, and torn edge matched exactly. The complete heading had once read:

> **ESCROW—17**

The burned fragment preserved only its final portion:

> **ROW—17**

On the ledger's flyleaf, Aldous had written in his own hand:

> "Statement returned by G.R. already scorched, 17 October. Obtain bank duplicate."

Gideon said a candle had overturned at his chambers and that the damage was accidental.

Felix recalled seeing Aldous detach the loose burned fragment from the statement before dinner and place it in his waistcoat pocket with his notes.

One more document altered the chronology. On October 16, the day after Ruth died, Aldous sent Gideon a written demand to bring every Escrow 17 voucher to Vesper House on October 18. Aldous wrote:

> "The discrepancies will be explained before the evening is finished."

Gideon therefore knew before the dinner—not merely after Aldous's announcement—that the account was about to be exposed.

## Part VI: The Intersection

After the tide reopened the causeway, the book and Ruth's clothing were examined professionally.

Inside the vellum book's hollow spine was a slender steel needle connected to the return spring. Pressing the clasp projected the needle through the empty groove; releasing it withdrew the needle completely into the spine.

The needle bore aconitine and the same blue-black marking dye found on Aldous's thumb and inside the clasp. A chemical examination found aconitine in Aldous's body.

The page block after the sixteenth leaf was modern folded paper installed during the recent rebinding. The old opening leaves supplied an apparently plausible title and imprint; the unread modern block supplied thickness around the hollow spine. Aldous's folded spectacles and sheathed paper knife showed that he had handled the object without beginning to read or cut the leaves.

A bookbinder determined that the animal glue securing the mechanism had cured for at least twenty-four hours before the parcel was delivered. The device had therefore been assembled no later than October 17—after Gideon received Aldous's warning about Escrow 17, but before the dinner announcement.

The mechanism followed Cassian's published court plans closely enough that anyone possessing a copy and ordinary workshop skill could have constructed it. No unique tool mark identified its maker.

The estate-office key register supplied a different piece of access evidence. It recorded that Aldous handed the duplicate poison-cabinet key to Gideon in June for the hazardous-material inventory. Gideon initialed its receipt. The return column was blank.

Felix's dated work diary established how the two seventeens first became confused. Its October 15 entry read:

> "5:35—R.D. comparing Row 17 restoration papers. 5:45—G.R. at her desk; asks what she has found. Notebook open."

Gideon admitted speaking with Ruth but denied reading more than the heading `ROW 17`.

The estate-office diary and carrier's book supplied the preparation window:

- On October 15, Gideon used the Fallowmere estate office alone shortly before Ruth's telegram was filed. Felix was documented at Vesper House at that time.
- On October 18, Gideon used the office alone from 2:45 until 3:30. At 3:35, a carrier collected the parcel addressed to Aldous. Felix was again at Vesper House.

Those records establish opportunity to type both items, not authorship by themselves.

Ruth's injuries included bruising inflicted before the freight wagon struck her, consistent with a struggle and a forceful push. Her death was not an ordinary accident.

Finally, a watchmaker examined Aldous's desk clock. Its balance staff had been bent by a fresh sideways shock. A jolt to the desk could stop it without breaking its glass. The chair's scrape ended against the desk immediately beneath the clock.

If Mrs. Wren's twelve-minute comparison was accurate, the clock stopped at approximately 9:39—the same time she heard the chair scrape. Merritt's continuous observation establishes that Gideon and Cassian were then in the billiard room.

The murderer therefore did not need to enter or leave the locked study at the time of death. The relevant opportunities occurred when the poison disappeared, when the mechanism was built, and when the parcel was prepared.

No single clue names Aldous's killer. The solution lies in the intersection of:

- knowledge of Ruth's supposed discovery;
- motive concerning Escrow 17;
- access to aconitine;
- access to the Thorn plans;
- opportunity to prepare the typed summons and parcel;
- and an alibi that covers the mechanism's delayed effect rather than its construction.

The evidence set is now complete.
