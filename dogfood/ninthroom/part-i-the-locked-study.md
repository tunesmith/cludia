# The Ninth Room

## Part I: The Locked Study

> **Fair-play note**
>
> Neutral narration of observable conditions is reliable. Anything attributed to a character is testimony unless independently corroborated. Documents contain the words described, but their authors and contents may deceive. There is no secret passage, disguised body, supernatural event, or unintroduced culprit.

Vesper House stood on a low tidal island off the village of Fallowmere. A stone causeway joined it to the mainland whenever the tide permitted. Fallowmere had a railway station with passenger platforms, a parcels office, a telegraph counter, and a freight yard. Greyhaven, one stop inland, was the nearest town.

On October 18, 1924, six guests crossed the causeway to dine with Aldous Bell, a collector of rare books:

- **Miriam Bell**, Aldous's niece and a pharmaceutical chemist.
- **Felix Harrow**, his private secretary.
- **Dr. Lenora Pike**, his physician and oldest friend.
- **Gideon Rusk**, solicitor to the Bell estate.
- **Cassian Vale**, an illusionist whose apparatus Aldous collected.
- **Beatrice Orr**, curator of the Vesper collection.

Constable Merritt was also present. He had arrived before dinner to return the effects of Ruth Dallow, Beatrice's assistant cataloguer, and to ask Aldous final questions about her death. Merritt was not one of the six guests. The tide covered the causeway before he could leave.

Ruth had died three nights earlier beneath a freight wagon at Fallowmere Station. The preliminary finding was accidental death.

At dinner, Aldous placed an unopened parcel beside his chair. Its brown wrapper bore a faded bookseller's label. He did not explain where it had come from.

At nine precisely, he set down his wineglass and addressed the table.

> “One object in my collection is false. One account in my ledger is false. And someone at this table made a corpse of Ruth Dallow. At half past ten, I shall name the object, the account, and the killer.”

No one answered him.

At 9:05, Aldous carried the parcel upstairs to his study. The room was known as the Ninth Room because its door bore a brass numeral **IX**.

He told the housekeeper, Mrs. Wren:

> “No one comes in until I ring twice.”

Mrs. Wren watched him enter and lock the door from inside.

At 9:18, the study's handbell rang once. Mrs. Wren carried up the hot water Aldous normally requested in the evening. Before she knocked, a voice she identified as Aldous's came through the door.

> “Leave it, Wren. I shall ring twice when I am ready.”

She left the tray on a corridor table. It remained there untouched.

At 10:30, Aldous had not appeared. The study door was still locked, and its only key could be seen in the inside of the keyhole. The windows were latched. Iron bars crossed the chimney.

Felix and Cassian broke down the door.

Aldous was dead at his desk, but he was not seated. His knees rested on the carpet and his upper body had fallen forward across the blotter. His right hand clutched his coat above the heart.

There was no obvious weapon. At the fleshy base of his left thumb was a tiny puncture surrounded by a faint blue-black stain.

The parcel lay open. It had contained a gray vellum book entitled *A Dissertation Concerning the Tides*, supposedly printed in 1681. Its brass clasp stood open.

Only the first sixteen leaves had been opened along their folded edges. The block behind them remained unopened.

Aldous's spectacles were folded inside their case.

His desk clock had stopped at 9:27. Its glass was intact.

The chair stood nearly three feet behind its ordinary position. A fresh scrape ran backward from the desk to its front legs.

The desk stood at right angles to the cold fireplace, with its left corner less than two feet from the fender. A burned fragment of ruled paper lay against the fender. The surviving text read:

> **ROW—17**

Beside Aldous's left forearm was an unfinished sentence:

> “The mistake was assuming that Ruth had discovered—”

No one admitted sending the parcel.
