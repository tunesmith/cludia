# The Ninth Room

## How to present the mystery

This is a six-part fair-play mystery intended for incremental analysis. Present one numbered part at a time. After each part, let the solver record clues, construct hypotheses, and ask clarification questions before continuing.

Do not provide the separate epilogue until the solver has read Part VI and either made an accusation or explicitly requested the solution.

Evidence follows these rules:

- Neutral narration of observable conditions is reliable.
- Anything attributed to a character remains testimony unless independently corroborated.
- Documents reliably contain the words described, but their authors and contents may be deceptive.
- No secret passage, disguised body, supernatural event, or unintroduced culprit is involved.
- After Part VI, every fact needed for the intended solution has appeared.

## Part I: The Locked Study

Vesper House stood on a low tidal island off the village of Fallowmere. A stone causeway connected it to the mainland whenever the tide permitted. Fallowmere had a railway station with passenger platforms, a parcels office, a telegraph counter, and a freight yard. Greyhaven, one stop inland, was the nearest town.

On October 18, 1924, six guests crossed the causeway to dine with Aldous Bell, a collector of rare books:

- **Miriam Bell**, Aldous's niece and a pharmaceutical chemist.
- **Felix Harrow**, his private secretary.
- **Dr. Lenora Pike**, his physician and oldest friend.
- **Gideon Rusk**, solicitor to the Bell estate.
- **Cassian Vale**, an illusionist whose apparatus Aldous collected.
- **Beatrice Orr**, curator of the Vesper collection.

Constable Merritt was also at the house. He had arrived before dinner to return the effects of Ruth Dallow, Beatrice's assistant cataloguer, and to ask Aldous some final questions about her death. Merritt was not one of the six guests. The tide covered the causeway before he could leave.

Ruth had died three nights earlier beneath a freight wagon at Fallowmere Station. The preliminary finding was accidental death.

At dinner, Aldous placed an unopened parcel beside his chair and addressed the table:

> "One object in my collection is false. One account in my ledger is false. And someone at this table made a corpse of Ruth Dallow. At half past ten, I shall name the object, the account, and the killer."

At 9:05, Aldous carried the parcel upstairs to his study, known as the Ninth Room because its door bore a brass numeral IX. He told the housekeeper, Mrs. Wren:

> "No one comes in until I ring twice."

Mrs. Wren watched him lock the door from inside.

At 9:18, the study's handbell rang once. Mrs. Wren carried up the hot water Aldous normally requested in the evening. Before she knocked, his voice came through the door:

> "Leave it, Wren. I shall ring twice when I am ready."

She left the tray on a corridor table. It remained there untouched.

At 10:30, Aldous had not appeared. The door was still locked, and its only key could be seen in the inside of the keyhole. The windows were latched. Iron bars crossed the chimney. Felix and Cassian broke down the door.

Aldous was dead at his desk.

There was no obvious weapon. His right hand pressed against his chest. At the fleshy base of his left thumb was a tiny puncture surrounded by a faint blue-black stain.

The parcel lay open. It had contained a gray vellum book entitled *A Dissertation Concerning the Tides*, supposedly printed in 1681. Its brass clasp stood open. Only the first sixteen leaves had been separated along their folded edges; the remaining leaves were still uncut.

Aldous's spectacles were folded inside their case. His ivory paper knife remained sheathed on the opposite side of the room.

His desk clock had stopped at 9:27. Its glass was intact, but the winding key would not turn.

A fresh scrape ran from the chair's ordinary position toward the desk. The chair's back rail now rested against the desk directly beneath the clock.

On the cold hearth lay a burned fragment of paper bearing the surviving text:

> **ROW—17**

Beneath Aldous's right hand was an unfinished sentence:

> "The mistake was assuming that Ruth had discovered—"

No one admitted sending the parcel.
